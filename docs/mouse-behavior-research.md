# Mouse behavior and cat play: evidence and implementation notes

Research checked 17 November 2026. This is a targeted review of primary studies, including recent 2024–2026 work, not a systematic review or proof that this game improves welfare. The policy is authored software inspired by observations. Its numerical thresholds, probabilities and timings are design choices, not fitted biological parameters.

## What the evidence supports

| Primary study | Observation or result | Design inference and limits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Schaeffer et al. (2024), [Application of scaling to mouse spontaneous movement](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38768689/), Behavioural Brain Research, DOI 10.1016/j.bbr.2024.115062 | Mouse movement showed relationships between speed, path curvature and travel distance across open-field ambulation, string pulling and ladder walking. | Slow before sharp turns, distinguish short investigative movements from longer runs, and avoid constant-speed jitter. Our steering formula is an approximation; this study does not specify screen-pixel speeds. |
| Black et al. (2026), [Naturalistic climbing reveals adaptive strategies for interlimb coordination in freely moving mice](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42181251/), iScience, DOI 10.1016/j.isci.2026.115901 | Freely climbing mice changed limb coordination with environmental demands; stance duty factor decreased with speed, and gaps prompted adaptation. | Foot timing should respond to speed and activity, not a fixed decorative cycle. Climbing-specific fore/hind coordination should not be copied uncritically into flat running. This is a recent source for future terrain animation, not validation of our current gait. |
| [Dopamine-mediated formation of a memory module in the nucleus accumbens for goal-directed navigation](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01770-9) (2024), Nature Neuroscience | Shelter experience supported directed escape. Mice could return to the previously learned shelter location after the shelter was changed or removed. | Remember a cover location and prefer a coherent return route instead of selecting a random exit on every event. A screen edge is our abstraction of shelter; the software does not reproduce neural mechanisms. |
| [The Presence of a Shelter in an Open Field Test Has Differential Effects on the Behavior and Stress Response of Two Mouse Species](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11959681/) (2025), Journal of Experimental Zoology Part A, DOI 10.1002/jez.2904 | Both mouse species used shelter. Shelter availability was associated with broader exploration, less wall-seeking and fewer freezing events in the reported comparison. | Give the mouse a retreat option while permitting exploration away from it. Constant wall-hugging is not a universal realism rule; context and species matter. |
| [Buffering effects of shelter and palatable foods mitigate fear responses in foraging wild mice](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-41952-y) (2026), Scientific Reports | The field study emphasizes shelter, food resources and seasonal context when interpreting risk-sensitive foraging in wild mice. | Balance threat memory with a reason to investigate. Do not model every mouse as perpetually maximally afraid. These were wild field mice and ecological conditions, not a touchscreen or a laboratory house-mouse animation calibration. |
| Clark, Hamilton and Whishaw (2006), [Motor activity and formation of home bases in mice](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16530235/), Physiology & Behavior, DOI 10.1016/j.physbeh.2006.01.026 | Contextual visual and tactile cues influenced home-base location, movement paths, speeds and stops. Mice organized excursions around home bases. | Stable cover and repeated excursions produce a more coherent agent than unrelated random destinations. Our tease/peek motif is an artistic interpretation, not a demonstrated intention by real mice to tease cats. |
| Hall, Bradshaw and Robinson (2002), [Object play in adult domestic cats: the roles of habituation and disinhibition](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0168159102001533), Applied Animal Behaviour Science, DOI 10.1016/S0168-1591(02)00153-3 | Repeated presentation of an unchanged toy reduced play; a changed toy could restore interest. The interval between presentations affected the response. | Vary movement motifs and offer breaks or different toys. This supports testing novelty, not an assertion that randomized animation prevents habituation or makes a game addictive. Physical toys and rendered mice are different stimuli. |
| Ellis and Wells (2008), [The influence of visual stimulation on the behaviour of cats housed in a rescue shelter](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0168159107003619), Applied Animal Behaviour Science | The experiment compared visual conditions in 125 shelter cats. Attention declined during extended daily presentation, indicating habituation; welfare benefits were not established by that study. | Let the owner choose when sessions end and avoid treating longer screen exposure as automatically better. No automatic timeout is required by this evidence. Screen attention is a measurable response, not proof of positive welfare. |
| Payne, Kleinberger and Hirskyj-Douglas (2025), [Look What the Cat Tapped In](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/365042/2/365042.pdf), ACI 2025, DOI 10.1145/3768539.3768541 | A five-month cat-café study involved 28 humans and 16 cats. Human enjoyment was similar across tablet and traditional-toy conditions, while toy interactions produced stronger reported connection. Cats differed in how they watched or touched screens. | Preserve local owner adjustment and observe the individual cat; remote controls are removed by current product direction. Count looking, following and voluntary return as well as tapping. Treat the game as one possible play activity; tap count alone is not a happiness measure. |

## Policy derived from that evidence

The implemented policy has hidden, peek, watch, creep, dart, retreat and traverse states, with temporary injury, contact and toss states. It chooses among alternatives after arrival and a variable dwell period rather than taking unrelated random turns every frame. Recent touch positions influence cover selection; fear decays over simulation time. These are design inferences from the evidence above, not validated estimates of rodent psychology.

Close taps prompt escape after a brief delay. Moderately distant taps cause a short freeze; repeated events can escalate to escape. Distant taps orient the stationary mouse toward the event. The game has no camera and cannot tell whether a touch came from a cat, person or other object. “Fear” and “curiosity” describe internal simulation variables, not measurements of the real cat.

Some direct hits cause a harmless flinch, freeze or dodge. Other hits retain the requested injury/defeat reactions. The harmless probability is configurable. Blood, squashing and injury are cosmetic game choices; none of the studies above establishes that those effects improve feline engagement.

Numeric defaults and ranges belong to `little-mouse/public/settings.js`; avoid treating
study-inspired design choices as experimentally fitted values.

The current behavior and contact contracts live in [behavior](behavior.md) and [touch interaction](touch-interaction.md); these notes own the evidence rather than every implementation parameter. Global speed, hide duration, mouse size/count and Hunt tempo remain separate controls. The approach through fully invisible space is faster than the visible peek so the cat does not face an accidentally empty screen. The exploration budget starts when the silhouette appears. A blocked dart yields to watch instead of pushing indefinitely; injury and manual steering retain priority. These safeguards address software failure modes, not biological rules.

## What to test with this cat

Use short, optional sessions and compare one setting at a time. Record the chosen preset, initial latency to look or approach, sustained tracking, taps, voluntary departure and voluntary return. A cat watching without tapping may still be interested; a larger tap count alone does not establish a better experience.

Useful comparisons are one mouse versus two, quiet observation versus rapid motion, longer versus shorter peeks, and occasional novelty versus repeating one motif. Keep the owner able to pause or end immediately. Rotate in ordinary physical play rather than treating a screen as a complete substitute. “Engaging and varied” is a defensible design objective; “addictive” and “scientifically proven enrichment” are not supported outcomes for this app.

Automated checks cover multi-minute seeded return cycles, slow approaches, viewport changes, collision obstruction, real stopping during watch, delayed contextual touch responses, harmless-hit recovery, injury priority and manual takeover. Passing them establishes implementation behavior only. It does not prove photorealism, cat preference or welfare efficacy.
