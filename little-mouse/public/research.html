Designing for a cat,
not a scoreboard.

This is an experimental screen toy. Research informs the controls, but does not establish that this game improves feline welfare or that photorealistic graphics are more engaging than simple targets.

Recent mouse-behavior research and implementation notes (2024–2026) ↗

Movement on screens: promising, not a recipe

Ellis and Wells (2008) randomly assigned 125 shelter cats to five viewing conditions. Cats watched animate and inanimate movement more than human footage or a blank screen, but overall viewing occupied only 6.10% of observation time and declined during daily exposure. This was passive television viewing, not touchscreen play; the three-hour exposure was a study procedure, not a recommended session length.

Design inference: keep the requested realistic 3D prey and aggressive motion, with adjustable bursts, straight runs, turns and pauses. Compare movement patterns independently of visual detail. The study does not establish that photorealism, maximum speed or continuous movement is best.

The influence of visual stimulation on the behaviour of cats housed in a rescue shelter · Applied Animal Behaviour Science 113, 166–174 · University publication record.

Novelty and habituation

Hall, Bradshaw and Robinson (2002) found that play declined with repeated presentation of an unchanged physical toy. A contrasting replacement renewed play. We translate that finding into owner-selectable prey appearances and movement presets, with pace controlled separately. Changing one feature at a time makes preferences easier to observe. This is a design inference: the study did not test procedural movement, digital backgrounds, or this game. Contrasting toy colors do not establish a universally preferred screen color or a schedule for switching stimuli.

Object play in adult domestic cats: the roles of habituation and disinhibition · Applied Animal Behaviour Science 79, 263–271.

Let the human follow the cat

Payne, Kleinberger and Hirskyj-Douglas (2025) compared tablet-based interaction with traditional toys in a five-month cat café study involving 28 human and 16 feline participants. Human enjoyment was similar across conditions; traditional toys produced a stronger sense of connection. Their tablet system allowed people to adjust playback speed in response to cats. Our resulting design recommendation is live steering, pacing, pause and end controls on both the iPad and phone companion. MewTube used curated video; it did not test remote phone control or rendered 3D prey. Human enjoyment is not evidence of feline enjoyment. Catch counts describe activity only; they do not measure enjoyment.

Look What the Cat Tapped In: Exploring Digital Interactive Systems Designed for the Cat Café Experience · ACI 2025.

Offer a finish to the chase

Kogan and Grigg (2021) found associations between reported laser-pointer use and some guardian-reported repetitive behaviors in a survey of 618 cat guardians. This correlational study cannot establish causation and did not test tablet games. It did not establish whether reported behaviors preceded laser use or identify a problematic duration threshold. Our design recommendation is a visible reaction at the contact location, a distinct catch state and owner-controlled pause/end controls. Requested blood effects remain a visual response option. Neither blood nor a digital catch was tested by this study, and neither is an established substitute for physical capture or a proven way to prevent frustration.

Laser Light Pointers for Use in Companion Cat Play: Association with Guardian-Reported Abnormal Repetitive Behaviors · Animals 11(8), 2178.

Observe engagement without inventing a welfare score

Product evaluation proposal: record looking, approaches, paw contacts, pauses, leaving and voluntary returns separately, using human observation where the device cannot measure them. A motionless cat may be watching; no touch does not necessarily mean disinterest. Keep owner ratings separate from observed behavior. Compare one setting at a time across repeated, voluntary sessions and vary the order. These are proposed evaluation methods, not validated measures of feline enjoyment.

What we are testing, not claiming

Stalking pauses, realistic fur, articulated legs, configurable floor colors and textures, and owner-controlled continuous play are product choices to test with your cat. None is presented as a scientifically proven optimum. Watch whether the cat chooses to engage, pauses, or walks away; adapt play rather than escalating stimulation automatically. Native iPad delivery, a phone companion and OTA updates remain product requirements, not conclusions from animal-behavior research. These notes recommend features; they do not certify implementation.
